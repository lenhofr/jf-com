Everyone treats the combine as an audition. Run fast, jump high, move up. That framing is not wrong, but it is small, and it causes players to give away things in February that they would like back in May.

What is actually happening in Indianapolis is an information exchange. Thirty-two organisations are trying to learn everything they can about you at the lowest possible cost, and you are trying to establish a position. The testing is the part on television. It is not the part that moves money.

## The medical is the event

Ask anyone who has worked a draft where the real risk sits, and they will tell you the same thing: the medical.

Every team's doctors examine you. Everything gets documented — old surgeries, the shoulder you played through, the thing you never told anyone about. That record is shared across clubs, and it follows you. A flag in that file can move a player further than a bad forty ever will, and unlike a bad forty, you cannot rerun it at a pro day.

This is why the preparation that matters starts months earlier. Getting imaging done in advance, understanding what is in your own file, knowing which questions are coming and having a straight answer ready — that is the work. Walking in and finding out what your medical says at the same time the team does is the worst version of this.

## The interview is a discovery process

Teams get a formal window with you, plus whatever informal contact they can arrange. They will ask about scheme. They will also ask about your family, your money, your friends, and the worst day of your life.

Some of this is genuine evaluation. Some of it is designed to see how you respond to being pushed. And some of it is straightforwardly gathering information that becomes useful later, when the same organisation is deciding what to offer you and how hard to push in negotiation.

You are allowed to be prepared for this. Being prepared is not being coached into a robot; it is knowing which stories you are going to tell about the hard parts of your life, and having decided in advance where the line is. Players get into trouble when they improvise on subjects they have never said out loud before.

## Why any of this matters after you are drafted

Here is the part that gets lost. Since the rookie wage scale came in, first contracts are slotted — where you are picked determines, within a narrow band, what you are paid. So people conclude that draft position is the only thing that matters and negotiation is dead.

Draft position is enormously important. But the slot is not the whole contract. Offset language, the timing of the signing bonus, workout bonus structures, and the specific language around guarantees still get negotiated, and how a team feels about you shapes how much friction you meet on each one. For first-round picks, there is also a fifth-year option sitting at the end of the deal, and the terms under which it can be exercised become one of the most consequential things in your professional life several years before anyone talks about it.

More than that: the file built on you in February is the file the organisation consults when it decides whether you are worth an extension, a restructure, or a release. That is a decade of downstream consequence from a week of interviews.

## The one thing I would tell a client

Do not optimise for the number on the stopwatch at the expense of the relationship and the record.

Run well. Of course run well. But understand that you are also being scouted on whether you are honest about the shoulder, whether your answers stay consistent across thirty-two rooms, and whether you seem like someone who will still be reliable at twenty-eight.

The forty is over in four and a half seconds. The file is permanent.
