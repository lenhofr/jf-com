Recruiting season produces a strange conversation. Agents show up with a deck, a list of clients, and a number they think you can get. Players ask about the fee and about who else the agent represents. Then a decision gets made, often on the basis of who was most impressive in a hotel conference room.

The fee is capped. It is three percent for contract advisory work under NFLPA rules, and any agent who tells you their fee is the differentiator is telling you something about themselves. So here are the questions I would ask instead, including the ones that are awkward to ask me.

## "How many clients do you have, and how many did you have last year?"

Roster size is the most useful number in this conversation, and the one least often volunteered.

There is no single correct answer — a large agency with real infrastructure can serve a big roster well, and a solo agent with too many clients cannot serve anyone. What you are listening for is whether the number is deliberate. An agent who can tell you their target roster size, and why, has thought about capacity. An agent whose roster grew forty percent last year and plans to grow again has just told you what your call volume is going to be like.

Ask about the trend, not only the total.

## "Who will actually be on my calls?"

At many agencies the person who recruits you is not the person who does your day-to-day. That arrangement can work perfectly well — but you should know about it before you sign, not in March when you cannot reach anyone.

Ask directly: who negotiates my contract, who handles marketing, who answers when I call on a Sunday, and are those the same person? If there is a team, ask to meet the team. If the answer is that the person in front of you does all of it, ask how that scales across the roster size they just quoted.

## "What is your average response time, and what is your worst?"

Everyone says they answer the phone. Ask for specifics. Ask what happens during draft week, when they are also managing eleven other clients. Ask whether anyone can act on your behalf if they are on a flight.

Then — and this is the part players skip — call two of their clients and ask the same question. Not the reference the agent offers you. Ask for the full client list and pick your own.

## "Tell me about a client relationship that ended badly"

Every agent who has been doing this for more than a few years has lost a client, and some of those separations were the agent's fault.

An agent who says it has never happened is either very new or not being straight with you. What you want is someone who can describe what went wrong, what their part in it was, and what they changed afterwards. That answer tells you more about how they will behave when your relationship is under strain than any success story will.

## "What do you think my market is, and what would make you wrong?"

The recruiting pitch usually contains a number. Push on it.

Ask what has to be true for that number to happen. Ask what the downside case is. Ask what they would say about your weaknesses if a team asked. An agent who only has an optimistic case has either not done the work or has done it and decided not to share it, and the second is worse.

The honest version of this answer is often unwelcome. Mine has cost me signatures. I would rather lose the signature than spend two years managing a client's disappointment about a number I let them believe.

## "Are you certified, and what else are you?"

Certification as a contract advisor is table stakes; verify it rather than assume. Beyond that, ask what else they bring. A law degree helps with contract language, and plenty of excellent agents do not have one. Someone who has run a business understands the operational side of a career differently than someone who has not.

None of these are requirements. They are facts you should have, because "he seemed sharp" is not a due diligence process for the person who will handle the most consequential financial decisions of your twenties.

## The meta-question

Notice which questions make the agent uncomfortable.

You are about to enter a multi-year relationship in which you will occasionally need to hear things you do not want to hear. If they cannot take a direct question in the meeting where they are trying to win you, they will not take one in year three.
