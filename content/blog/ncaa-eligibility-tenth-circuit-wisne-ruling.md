**On November 6, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reversed the lower-court injunction in *Wisne v. NCAA*, handing the NCAA its biggest legal victory in the ongoing fight over its new five-year, age-based eligibility model — but the ruling does not end the litigation, and a Kentucky case involving more than 30 athletes is still very much alive.**

For families of college athletes — especially those in the high school Class of 2022 who exhausted four years of eligibility before this school year — this decision deserves a careful read.

## What is the NCAA's age-based eligibility model?

On June 23, 2026, the NCAA formally adopted the "age-based eligibility model." [Under that model](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/college/2026/08/21/ncaa-fifth-year-eligibility-stay-federal-court-what-it-means/91404074007/), athletes now have five years to compete in up to five full seasons. The eligibility clock starts at an athlete's 19th birthday or the date of first full-time college enrollment, whichever comes first.

The critical catch: the NCAA did not apply the new model retroactively to athletes who graduated high school in 2022 and had already used their four years under the old rules. That decision sparked a wave of lawsuits across the country.

## What happened in federal court?

On July 31, 2026, U.S. District Court Judge Caroline Sweeney in Colorado issued a nationwide injunction, giving blanket fifth-year eligibility to the entire 2022 high school graduating class. The NCAA appealed and, on August 21, the Tenth Circuit granted a stay by a 2-1 vote.

On November 6, the Tenth Circuit went further: it [reversed and vacated the district court's injunction entirely](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/ncaaf/2026/11/06/ncaa-scores-major-court-win-over-five-year-eligibility-rule-wisne/92647633007/). NCAA chief legal officer Scott Bearby described the decision as consistent with every federal court of appeals that had considered the merits of similar injunctions.

Sports Business Journal reported that [the ruling turned on the plaintiffs' failure to define the relevant market](https://www.sportsbusinessjournal.com/Articles/2026/11/08/ncaa-scores-major-legal-victory-in-battle-over-five-year-eligibility-rule/) — a necessary step in establishing antitrust harm. Without that showing, the court held the extraordinary remedy of a preliminary injunction was unwarranted. One legal expert called it a "huge win" and "unquestionably the association's biggest legal victory yet" in the eligibility fight.

## Why the ruling does not end the dispute

Here is what matters most right now: the Tenth Circuit ruling in *Wisne* addresses only that federal case. Sports Business Journal noted the decision ["does nothing to tamp down dozens of similar lawsuits playing out in state courts across the country"](https://www.sportsbusinessjournal.com/Articles/2026/11/08/ncaa-scores-major-legal-victory-in-battle-over-five-year-eligibility-rule/) — approximately 200 state cases involving as many as 850 athletes.

This patchwork has defined the eligibility crisis all year. As attorney Ryan Regula explained after the August stay, [the situation creates a "two-tier system"](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/college/2026/08/21/ncaa-fifth-year-eligibility-stay-federal-court-what-it-means/91404074007/) where athletes with independent court orders can still compete while those relying solely on the federal class-wide protection cannot.

## The active Kentucky case

There is an ongoing lawsuit in Jefferson County Circuit Court involving more than 30 athletes — and [it remains alive](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/louisville-kentucky-athletes-remain-eligible-142028443.html) despite the Tenth Circuit's federal ruling.

Athletes in that case include Louisville basketball players Seth Trimble and LJ Wells, Louisville football players Jabari Mack and Kalib Perry, and Kentucky football player Alex Afari Jr. Judge Brian Edwards granted a temporary injunction on October 16 after an evidentiary hearing at which Trimble and his coach testified. On October 30, Kentucky Court of Appeals Judge Sara Walter Combs denied the NCAA's request for emergency relief, writing that ["the NCAA has not shown that there is an imminent, ruinous, and incalculable injury"](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/louisville-kentucky-athletes-remain-eligible-142028443.html) requiring a stay. Those athletes remain eligible while a three-judge panel considers the NCAA's request for interlocutory relief.

The November 6 federal ruling does not dissolve the Kentucky injunction. State-court proceedings follow their own procedural path.

## What about the Protect College Sports Act?

The Senate-passed Protect College Sports Act, which I discussed in [an earlier post](/protect-college-sports-act-nil-agent-fees), would cap NCAA eligibility at five years as part of a broader federal framework. But the bill's prospects dimmed sharply after the November 3 midterms. Golfweek/USA TODAY Sports reported that [one political scientist now puts the bill's chances of passing the current lame-duck House at around 5%](https://golfweek.usatoday.com/story/sports/golf/college/2026/11/04/protect-college-sports-act-news-pcsa-elections-2026-congress-college-golf/92617046007/), with Democrats set to take control of both chambers on January 3. Any new legislation would start over from scratch.

The courts remain the primary arena for resolving these eligibility questions — at least for now.

## What should athletes and families keep in mind?

A few practical points for Class of 2022 athletes navigating this:

- **Your eligibility depends on which court order covers you.** The Tenth Circuit vacated the federal *Wisne* injunction. If you were relying solely on that class-wide protection, your status has changed. A separate state-court order is a different matter.
- **State outcomes vary by jurisdiction.** Kentucky, Ohio, and Arizona have each seen different results. What happened in one state does not automatically apply in another.
- **Timing is critical on both sides.** Playing while ineligible can have serious consequences for the athlete and the school. Sitting out when a valid court order grants eligibility means losing time that cannot be recovered.

Every athlete's situation — which class they enrolled in, which state they compete in, which court orders exist — is different. **This post provides general information, not legal advice; anyone facing an eligibility question should seek individualized counsel for their specific circumstances.**

## The bigger picture

NCAA chief legal officer Scott Bearby's memo criticized "serial lawsuits and forum shopping" and called the patchwork of state injunctions "unacceptable." The Tenth Circuit gave the NCAA its strongest legal endorsement yet. But the instability that produced these lawsuits has not gone away: roughly 850 athletes are still litigating, Congress appears unlikely to provide a statutory fix before the new year, and programs, coaches, and families are making real decisions under conditions that remain unsettled.

For athletes, the lesson that keeps repeating itself: understand exactly what protects your eligibility, and do not assume that national news coverage about a federal ruling tells you what applies in your state or your case.
