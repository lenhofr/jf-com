I have now read a lot of collegiate NIL agreements, and the same handful of problems keep appearing. Not because anyone is running a scam — mostly because these documents are being adapted from ordinary influencer templates by people who have never had to think about what happens when an eighteen-year-old becomes famous four years later.

Here is what I look for first, in order.

## The word "perpetual"

A licence to use your name, image, and likeness should have an end date. Many of the agreements I see do not have one. They grant rights "in perpetuity," sometimes "throughout the universe," sometimes "in all media now known or hereafter devised."

That last phrase is not a joke. It is standard entertainment-industry drafting, and in a film contract it makes sense. In a deal paying a college sophomore a few thousand dollars for four social posts and an appearance, it means that company can keep using the photographs it took of you, forever, including in the year you sign an NFL contract and your likeness is worth a great deal more.

Strike it. The licence should run for the term of the agreement plus a short, defined tail — enough time to sell through existing inventory and let already-placed campaigns finish. Ninety days to a year, depending on the category. Not forever.

## Exclusivity that is broader than the deal

Watch for exclusivity defined by category, and then watch how the category is defined.

"Beverages" sounds narrow until you realise it takes you out of water, sports drinks, energy drinks, coffee, and juice for the entire term, for one deal with a soda company. "Apparel" can swallow your ability to do anything with a shoe company. If a brand wants category exclusivity, it should pay for the category, and the category should be written as narrowly as the money justifies.

Also check whether the exclusivity survives the term. It should not.

## Sublicensing and assignment

Two clauses that sound administrative and are not.

**Sublicensing** lets the company give your likeness to someone else. If a brand can sublicense freely, you have effectively signed with every partner it has, including ones you would have refused. **Assignment** lets the company transfer the whole agreement to a different company — so the local business you liked and wanted to support can sell your deal to someone you have never met.

You do not need to ban either outright. You need consent rights. "Not without the athlete's prior written consent" is a short phrase that does a lot of work.

## Renewal that happens without you

Automatic renewal with a narrow opt-out window is common and rarely favourable. If a deal renews unless you give notice between sixty and thirty days before the end of a term you have forgotten about, you will renew it by accident. Ask for renewal by mutual written agreement, or at minimum extend the window and require the company to remind you in writing.

## The morals clause that only points one way

Most of these agreements let the brand terminate if you do something embarrassing. Fine — that is a legitimate business concern, though the standard should be objective rather than "in the company's sole discretion."

Far fewer let _you_ terminate if the brand does something embarrassing. You are lending your name to their reputation as much as they are borrowing yours. The clause should be reciprocal.

## What this is really about

The money in most collegiate NIL deals is not large enough to justify a fight over any single clause. That is exactly why the bad terms survive: everyone reasonably concludes it is not worth the argument.

But the terms are not priced against today's value. They are priced against the value of your name at the peak of your career, because that is when someone will want to use the rights you signed away as a sophomore. The asymmetry is the whole problem.

Have someone read it. Not because the company is trying to trap you — usually it isn't — but because nobody on the other side of the table is being paid to protect the version of you that exists in eight years.
