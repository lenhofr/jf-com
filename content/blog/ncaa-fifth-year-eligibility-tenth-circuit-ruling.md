**On November 6, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reversed a nationwide injunction that had given Class of 2022 college athletes a path to a fifth season. The ruling is a clear win for the NCAA, but it does not end the litigation, and athletes competing under state court orders may still be playing through this week.**

## What is the age-based eligibility model?

The NCAA adopted a new age-based eligibility model in June 2026. Under the model, current and future athletes [get five years to compete in up to five full seasons](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/ncaaf/2026/11/06/ncaa-scores-major-court-win-over-five-year-eligibility-rule-wisne/92647633007/), with the eligibility clock starting at a player's 19th birthday or the date they enroll at a university, whichever happens first.

The catch: the NCAA did not make the rule retroactive. Athletes who graduated high school in 2022 and had already used four years of eligibility were told their careers were over under the new framework. The Class of 2022 pushed back in court, arguing the NCAA owed them the same five-year window it was now granting to younger athletes.

## What happened in Wisne v. NCAA?

Brock Wisne, a former Northern Colorado basketball player, became the named plaintiff in a class-action lawsuit filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Colorado. [In July 2026, Judge Charlotte Sweeney granted a nationwide preliminary injunction](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/tenth-circuit-rules-favor-ncaa-155503008.html?src_src=partner&src_cmp=whatfinger) in favor of the class, effectively making the entire Class of 2022 eligible for a fifth season. The result was immediate chaos, with programs scrambling to add players who had already been released or moved on.

The NCAA asked the Tenth Circuit to put that injunction on hold. In August 2026, the appellate court granted the stay, pausing the injunction while the appeal worked its way through the system. Oral arguments were held October 13.

On November 6, the three-judge panel, in a decision written by Judge Carolyn McHugh, [reversed the district court's ruling and vacated the injunction in full](https://www.chicagotribune.com/2026/11/06/ncaa-wins-appeal-5-year-eligibility-rule/). The central issue was market definition. The court found the plaintiffs had not shown that all Division I athletes, across every sport, competed in one unified market. As Judge McHugh wrote: ["Obviously a football player looking for a Division I roster spot would not view the 101 schools without a football program as reasonably good substitutes."](https://www.chicagotribune.com/2026/11/06/ncaa-wins-appeal-5-year-eligibility-rule/)

Without a properly defined market, the antitrust claims underlying the injunction could not hold, at least at this stage.

## Why the ruling does not end the fight

The Tenth Circuit's decision is significant, but the litigation is nowhere near finished. [Around 200 state court cases involving as many as 850 athletes remain active across the country.](https://www.chicagotribune.com/2026/11/06/ncaa-wins-appeal-5-year-eligibility-rule/) Many of those athletes are currently playing under injunctions issued by state judges, and a federal appellate ruling does not automatically dissolve a state court order.

The NCAA's chief legal officer Scott Bearby acknowledged that reality in a memo to membership after the ruling: ["While we are appreciative of the Tenth Circuit's decision, injunctions like the one entered by the district court have created chaos for the 2026-27 season."](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/ncaaf/2026/11/06/ncaa-scores-major-court-win-over-five-year-eligibility-rule-wisne/92647633007/) Bearby also criticized what he called "serial lawsuits and forum shopping by former student-athletes who repeatedly file suit in different jurisdictions in search of any judge willing to enter an injunction on a limited record."

The NCAA has won in some state-level cases, including in Kentucky, Arizona, and Ohio. But other state courts have continued to grant relief to individual athletes, creating a season where a player can be eligible on Tuesday and sidelined by Friday depending on what a court does that week.

## The Kentucky piece

The eligibility chaos is not abstract for Kentucky fans. [Jefferson County Circuit Court Judge Brian Edwards granted a temporary injunction on October 16](https://sportsbookwire.usatoday.com/story/sports/2026/10/16/ncaa-eligibility-lawsuit-fifth-year-seth-trimble-louisville-basketball-alex-afari-kentucky-football/92226118007/) to more than 30 Class of 2022 athletes, including Louisville basketball guard Seth Trimble, Louisville football players Jabari Mack and Kalib Perry, and Kentucky football linebacker Alex Afari Jr.

That ruling came after a Kentucky Court of Appeals panel dissolved an earlier injunction on October 2 on procedural grounds, finding that the original order should have been a temporary injunction rather than a temporary restraining order. The appellate panel, which included Judges Sara Walter Combs, Christopher McNeill, and Will Moynahan, held that granting Class of 2022 athletes a fifth season would "disrupt the status quo" because the new age-based model "did not, as the circuit court surmised, 'bar' them from doing anything they previously had a right to do."

Judge Edwards held a four-hour evidentiary hearing on October 14, where Trimble, Afari, Perry, and Louisville coach Pat Kelsey all testified. In his October 16 ruling, Edwards found the NCAA had "violated the covenant of good faith and fair dealing" by granting fifth and sixth seasons to some similarly situated athletes through its waiver process while denying others without consistent explanation. ["The Court can reach no other conclusion other than that this is arbitrary,"](https://sportsbookwire.usatoday.com/story/sports/2026/10/16/ncaa-eligibility-lawsuit-fifth-year-seth-trimble-louisville-basketball-alex-afari-kentucky-football/92226118007/) Edwards wrote.

Attorneys for the Jefferson County athletes noted that the Tenth Circuit's ruling in the Wisne antitrust case would have no direct bearing on the Jefferson County contract-based claims. [The Jefferson County athletes were arguing breach of contract, not antitrust violations.](https://sportsbookwire.usatoday.com/story/sports/2026/10/16/ncaa-eligibility-lawsuit-fifth-year-seth-trimble-louisville-basketball-alex-afari-kentucky-football/92226118007/) The two tracks of litigation are distinct.

One of the Louisville athletes caught in this back-and-forth, LJ Wells, played his first four college seasons at Northern Kentucky University before transferring to Louisville. His situation captures exactly the kind of career disruption that is playing out for players across the country right now.

## What this means for athletes and families

The practical stakes are real. Alex Afari played two games for Kentucky, recorded 11 tackles, then had to watch two overtime wins from the sideline as the legal proceedings shifted. As Afari told the Jefferson County court on October 14: ["I will never get (those games) back."](https://sportsbookwire.usatoday.com/story/sports/2026/10/16/ncaa-eligibility-lawsuit-fifth-year-seth-trimble-louisville-basketball-alex-afari-kentucky-football/92226118007/)

For families trying to understand what their athlete can or cannot do right now, a few points matter:

**The Tenth Circuit ruling vacated the Wisne nationwide injunction.** Class of 2022 athletes who were relying on that federal injunction for eligibility have lost that legal cover.

**State court injunctions are separate.** Whether a particular athlete is eligible today depends on which court they filed in, what that court ordered, and whether that order has been appealed or dissolved. No single ruling governs every athlete.

**The legal theory matters.** The Wisne case was an antitrust claim. Some state cases, including the Jefferson County case in Kentucky, are contract claims. The Tenth Circuit's antitrust analysis does not automatically knock out a contract argument.

**Nothing is final.** Plaintiffs in Wisne could seek further review. The Jefferson County case continues. New lawsuits will likely be filed before basketball season moves into full swing. As Louisville's coach Kelsey put it from the witness stand: ["We don't think there are rules right now."](https://sportsbookwire.usatoday.com/story/sports/2026/10/16/ncaa-eligibility-lawsuit-fifth-year-seth-trimble-louisville-basketball-alex-afari-kentucky-football/92226118007/)

Congress could resolve the uncertainty. The Protect College Sports Act, which addresses eligibility among other issues, passed the Senate 77-22 in late September but faces a complicated path through the House. No legislative fix is imminent.

For now, Class of 2022 athletes and their families should track their specific case, confirm the status of any injunction they rely on, and get advice tailored to their individual situation before making any decisions about playing, transferring, or signing anything.
