**The SEC is actively discussing a luxury tax on schools that spend beyond the House v. NCAA revenue-sharing cap, with a possible conference vote before the early signing period in December. Nothing is final — but the proposal signals where college athletics is heading, and athletes, agents, and businesses tied to NIL deals should be paying attention.**

## What is the SEC actually considering?

According to reporting by ESPN's Pete Thamel and Mark Schlabach, a working group of SEC athletic directors, university presidents, and general counsels has been discussing a luxury-tax framework for several weeks. [The conference could vote in the near future, with the goal of having something in place before the early signing period on December 2–4, 2026.](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/stop-playing-games-sec-reportedly-234120118.html)

Under the proposal, schools that spend more on athletes than the revenue-sharing cap allows would pay a graduated tax on the excess. One formula under discussion would tax spending at an increasing rate — for example, 1% on the first million over the cap and 3% on the third million over. [Some schools want the penalty to stay as low as possible, reasoning that a modest penalty would encourage programs to report overspending rather than hide it.](https://www.essentiallysports.com/ncaa-college-football-news-sec-considers-luxury-tax-on-college-football-programs-that-overspend-on-players-report/)

The SEC has not determined what it would do with revenue collected. Options under discussion include funding the College Sports Commission, establishing a post-graduate medical fund for former athletes, and covering legal costs from settlement litigation. [The conference reportedly does not favor copying MLB's approach of redistributing tax money to lower-revenue teams.](https://ngscsports.com/sec-eyes-luxury-tax-on-nil-spending/)

## Why the cap has become the floor

Under the [House v. NCAA settlement](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/salary-cap-college-football-nil-192853841.html), schools can share revenue directly with athletes up to $21.3 million in 2026–27, rising to approximately $22.2 million in 2027–28. That limit covers all athletes across all sports — not just football.

Third-party NIL deals — money routed through booster collectives and outside businesses — sit entirely outside that cap. NIL company Opendorse found that the average Power Four school offered athletes $13.5 million in third-party deals above the revenue-sharing cap during the system's first year. Iowa State athletic director Jamie Pollard said it plainly: ["We entered into a billion dollar settlement within college athletics that said there was going to be a cap in revenue sharing and the cap became the floor."](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/salary-cap-college-football-nil-192853841.html)

That gap is what the SEC is targeting. [One source told ESPN: "We're living in this world where we have no idea what anybody's spending. Agents can play you all day long because there is no general database."](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/stop-playing-games-sec-reportedly-234120118.html)

## The Big Ten already moved

The SEC is not acting alone. [On October 28, 2026, the Big Ten's presidents and chancellors unanimously approved a rule setting a $49 million cap on what each of its 18 member institutions can spend on athletes](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/big-ten-makes-major-nil-225545392.html) — combining the $21.6 million revenue-share cap with a $27.5 million ceiling on school involvement in arranging third-party NIL agreements. The Big Ten attached escalating financial penalties for violations and framed the rule as preparation for the anticipated passage of the Protect College Sports Act.

The SEC is developing a different approach rather than mirroring the Big Ten model, but both conferences are moving toward tighter financial oversight at the conference level regardless of what Congress ultimately does.

## What this means for recruiting and athletes

A luxury tax reshapes recruiting in ways that are not immediately obvious.

**For well-resourced programs:** A modest graduated tax is unlikely to be a deterrent. If the penalty for spending $3 million over the cap works out to a small fraction of an athletic department's overall budget, some programs will simply build it into their recruiting costs and keep spending. That concern is already being voiced inside conference discussions.

**For mid-tier SEC programs:** Transparency may actually help. If every program can see what competitors are actually paying, the arms-race dynamic of guessing — and over-bidding to beat rumors — becomes less distorting. Programs that were overspending to stay ahead of exaggerated competing offers may find they were not as far behind as they assumed.

**For athletes in the transfer portal:** This is where athletes and their representatives need to be most careful. If the tax pushes more compensation into the direct, reported category and discourages off-the-books third-party arrangements, the structure of offers will change. Athletes evaluating transfer portal opportunities should understand what is actually guaranteed versus what is informally promised — and whether the school's involvement in any outside deal could create compliance exposure later.

## What it means for NIL deals

A luxury tax does not directly cap third-party NIL deals. It targets revenue sharing above the settlement cap. But the SEC's stated goal of reducing third-party "money washing" could push schools to consolidate compensation into direct payments that are counted and reported.

For businesses currently sponsoring athletes through school-facilitated collective arrangements, the structure and documentation of those deals will matter more. A deal informally facilitated by an athletic department may find itself reclassified — and counted against a spending threshold. Athletes and businesses entering NIL contracts should understand which category their arrangement falls into before signing, not after.

## Where enforcement stands now

No SEC luxury tax is in effect. The proposal is still under discussion. [The College Sports Commission is already testing its enforcement power under the existing settlement framework, having questioned between five and ten Power Four programs about possible cap violations tied to third-party payments.](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/salary-cap-college-football-nil-192853841.html) The Commission has authority to issue postseason bans and restrict a school's spending under the revenue-sharing cap.

That enforcement backdrop makes the SEC's timing meaningful. A conference-level luxury tax would add accountability alongside what the Commission is already doing — and potentially a more predictable one.

## The real goal: making spending visible

The tax mechanism is almost secondary to the stated goal. If the proposal works as described, the most significant output is a shared record of actual roster spending that every conference member can see.

I have written before about how the lack of transparency in NIL and transfer portal markets allows bad actors to exploit athletes — agents charging excessive fees, programs making promises they don't intend to keep, and no external check on any of it. A system that requires reporting does not fix all of those problems, but it removes one of the most effective tools bad actors rely on: information asymmetry.

Athletes deserve to negotiate with accurate information. So do schools. Whatever its deterrent effect on overspending, a luxury tax that creates real reporting obligations points the SEC in the right direction.
