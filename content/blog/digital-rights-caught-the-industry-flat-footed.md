I led a digital rights division when that was an unusual thing for an agency to have. A lot of what we worked on in that period has since collapsed, been rebuilt, or been renamed. The specific products mattered less than what the exercise exposed: the industry did not have a coherent framework for licensing an athlete's likeness into a digital context, and in many respects it still does not.

## The problem was never the technology

Every few years a new format arrives that wants to use athlete likeness — trading platforms, in-game skins, collectibles, and now synthetic media. Each time, the industry treats it as a novel question. It is not. It is the same question with new packaging: who granted what rights, for how long, in which territories, and what happens to the asset when the grant expires?

The reason it keeps feeling novel is that the contracts were never written to survive a format change. Agreements drafted for print and broadcast got extended by analogy into digital, usually with a catch-all phrase, and the catch-all did the heavy lifting nobody negotiated.

"In all media now known or hereafter devised" is the clause that has done the most damage to athlete likeness value over the last twenty years, and almost nobody argued about it at signing, because at signing it was hypothetical.

## What digital changes in practice

Three things behave differently once likeness goes into a digital product, and all three are contractual rather than technical.

**Permanence.** A television commercial stops airing. A digital asset does not necessarily stop existing. If a licence expires but the artifact persists in a marketplace, in a game, or on a chain, the practical question becomes who is obligated to do what — and most agreements are silent, because the drafter never imagined a product that outlives its own licence.

**Derivation.** Digital products get remixed. An image becomes an animation, becomes a model, becomes a training input. Each step is arguably a derivative work, and whether your grant covered derivatives is usually decided by a single word you did not notice.

**Scale without renegotiation.** Traditional licensing had natural friction — a new campaign meant a new conversation. Digital distribution removes that friction. The same grant can be exploited across many more surfaces at no marginal cost, which means the value of a broad grant to the licensee has gone up dramatically while the price paid to the athlete has not.

## Where the industry got caught

Agencies were slow to build the specialism, and clubs and platforms moved faster than representation did. For a period the people negotiating these deals on the athlete's side were, frankly, learning the vocabulary during the negotiation. I include myself in that. Being early is uncomfortable; it also means making mistakes in public.

The lasting cost was structural. A generation of agreements got signed with grants far broader than the consideration justified, and those grants are still operative. When someone tells you likeness value in sports is under-monetised, part of what they are describing is not a market failure. It is the accumulated effect of a lot of poorly drafted licences.

## What I do differently now

I ask for a rights inventory before I ask about price.

What has already been granted, to whom, for how long, and with what carve-outs? Most athletes cannot answer this, and neither can their prior representation, because nobody kept a register. Building one is unglamorous and it is the single highest-value hour of work available on most of these files.

Then, in new agreements: define the media. Enumerate it, and put a term on the enumeration. Require consent for derivative works and for sublicensing. Address what happens to persistent assets at expiry, explicitly, even if the answer is imperfect. And price breadth — if a licensee wants a wide grant, that is a legitimate ask and it should cost more than a narrow one.

## The next one is already here

Synthetic likeness is the current version of this problem, and it is a harder one, because the thing being licensed is no longer a recording of you but a capacity to generate you. The agreements I am seeing handle it about as well as the early digital agreements handled digital, which is to say by analogy and with a catch-all.

The lesson from the last cycle is not that we should have predicted the technology. It is that the contracts should have been written so that we did not have to.
