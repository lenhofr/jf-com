The number that gets reported is the number the team is happy to have reported. It is almost never the number that reaches your account. The gap between the two is not spin, and it is not anyone lying to a reporter. It is contract language, and most of it sits in three places.

## "Guaranteed" is three separate promises

When people say a deal is fully guaranteed, they are compressing three different protections into one word.

A **skill guarantee** means you still get paid if the team decides you are not good enough. An **injury guarantee** means you still get paid if you cannot pass a physical. A **cap guarantee** means you still get paid if the team simply needs the room for someone else.

A contract can carry one, two, or all three, and a deal missing even one of them behaves very differently in the situation where you actually need it. A player with an injury guarantee but no skill guarantee is protected against the hit that ends his season and unprotected against the coordinator who decides he has lost a step. Those are not the same risk, and they do not arrive on the same schedule.

The second question is _when_. Money can be guaranteed at signing, or guaranteed later — vesting on a date that has to arrive first. A deal described as carrying a large guarantee may only guarantee a fraction of it the day you sign, with the rest vesting each subsequent March, conditional on you still being on the roster. If the team releases you before that date, the money that was going to vest never does. It was real. It just never became yours.

## Offset language decides whether you can be paid twice

Offset language is the clause I get asked about least and think about most.

Suppose you are released with guaranteed money still owed. You sign somewhere else. With offset language, your original team subtracts what your new team pays you from what it still owes. Without it, you collect both.

Teams will describe offsets as a technicality, and for most players it never comes up. But it only matters in exactly one scenario — the one where you have been released and are trying to rebuild value on a smaller deal — and in that scenario it is worth a great deal. The absence of offset language is one of the clearest signals of real negotiating leverage in a contract, precisely because it costs the team nothing on the day of signing and everything on the day it matters.

## Injury protection is narrower than it sounds

Injury guarantees are usually tied to a specific standard: you failed a physical, and the failure is attributable to a football injury sustained while performing services under the contract. That is a narrower category than "I got hurt."

Injuries that happen away from the facility, aggravations of a condition that predates the deal, and disputes about whether you are actually unable to perform all live in that gap. So do disagreements between the team's medical staff and your own. The clause that governs who decides, and what happens when the two doctors disagree, is worth reading before you need it. By the time you need it, it is not negotiable.

## What to actually ask

If you take nothing else from this: the useful questions are not about the total.

Ask how much is guaranteed **at signing**, not in aggregate. Ask which of the three guarantees applies to each tranche. Ask what vests, and on what date. Ask whether there is offset language, and if there is, ask what it would cost to remove it. Ask what happens to the guarantee if you are placed on a non-football injury list.

Then ask your agent to walk you through the worst realistic year of the deal — not the best one. The best year takes care of itself. The structure exists for the other one.

A headline number is a marketing document. The contract is the agreement. When those two disagree, and they usually do, only one of them is enforceable.
