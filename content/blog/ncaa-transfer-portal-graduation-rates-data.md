**New NCAA research released November 11, 2026, found that college athletes who transfer more than once graduate at rates 15 to 20 percentage points below their peers who never transfer.** For athletes and families weighing a portal entry, those numbers deserve as much attention as the NIL offer on the table.

## What did the NCAA actually find?

The NCAA research department combined its own enrollment records with data from the National Student Clearinghouse to build six-year academic histories for nearly 36,000 Division I student-athletes who started college in 2018-19. The sample broke down into roughly 24,500 athletes with no transfer record, about 7,700 who transferred once, and about 3,800 who attended three or more schools.

The [findings published on NCAA.org](https://www.ncaa.org/news/report-reveals-decline-in-graduation-rates-for-multiple-transfers/) show a consistent step-down pattern:

- **88%** of athletes who never transferred earned a bachelor's degree within six years.
- **81%** of athletes who transferred once graduated within six years.
- **71%** of athletes who transferred more than once graduated within six years.

The gap held regardless of race, gender, or sport. For FBS football specifically, 85% of non-transfers graduated on time compared to 58% of multiple transfers, a 27-point difference. Among Black student-athletes, non-transfers graduated at 82% while the rate for multiple transfers fell to 62%.

The NCAA said it will continue monitoring academic progress and degree completion while encouraging schools to support athletes in staying on track toward their degrees.

## Why this matters beyond the headline numbers

Graduation rates capture something that gets lost in the portal conversation: the degree itself has long-term value that an NIL dollar figure cannot replace. For most college athletes, professional sports will not be a career option, and the credential they carry out of school shapes what comes after.

The numbers also raise a harder question. When a transfer happens because an athlete genuinely needs a better fit, a healthier environment, or a program that develops their position, the move can be the right call. When it happens because an agent stands to earn more from a higher collective offer, the athlete may be absorbing a real academic cost that nobody put on the table during recruiting.

That concern is not new for me. In February 2025, I told SB Nation that too many people working in the NIL and transfer space are focused on "the short-term financial implications and their own benefit in the process and not about what's best for the athlete's future in the sport." The graduation data gives that concern a concrete number.

## What NCAA president Charlie Baker said to Congress

NCAA president Charlie Baker cited this research in a letter to Congress earlier this fall, [reported by Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/ncaa-president-warns-congress-transfer-180856087.html). Baker wrote that "the court-imposed unlimited transfer policy is leading to worse academic outcomes for student-athletes who transfer multiple times" and urged lawmakers to pass the Protect College Sports Act before the new Congress is seated in January.

Critics of the PCSA, including the Congressional Black Caucus and the NAACP, have argued the bill gives the NCAA and its top-revenue schools too much control and could disproportionately limit the earning power and mobility of minority athletes. That tension is real, and Congress should weigh it. The graduation data does not resolve the policy debate, but it does give athletes and families a concrete reason to think carefully before treating a second or third portal entry as a routine career move.

## What this means for athletes and families making portal decisions

The research describes population-level outcomes. It cannot tell any individual athlete what will happen if they transfer. But it identifies a risk that deserves honest discussion before a family makes a decision.

A few questions worth asking before entering the portal:

**On the academic side:** Will my credits transfer in full? How many semesters of athletic eligibility do I have left, and is that enough time to finish my degree at the new school? Does the school have a strong academic support program for athletes?

**On the representation side:** Is the person advising this move being paid a commission on the NIL deal that results from it? If so, their financial interest points in one direction regardless of your situation. That is not a reason to distrust everyone in the space, but it is a reason to get a clear answer to the question.

**On the football side:** What is the coach's track record of developing players at my position toward the professional level? A larger NIL number at a program where I will play less, or in a less prominent system, may cost more in draft stock than it pays in guaranteed cash.

**On the contract side:** How much of the offer is guaranteed, and over what timeline? What happens if I am injured, lose the starting role, or the coaching staff changes?

The portal is a tool, not an outcome. The athletes who use it well tend to treat the full picture, academic progress, development, fit, and contract terms, as a package rather than pricing only one variable.

## The larger pattern

The 2018-19 cohort the NCAA studied entered college before NIL was legal and before the portal became what it is today. The transfer rates in that sample are likely lower than what a 2022-23 or 2023-24 cohort would show. That means the gap in outcomes could widen in future research.

No single data release settles the ongoing legal and legislative fight over transfer rules. But for a family sitting across from an advisor who is pushing a second transfer, 71% versus 88% is a number worth having in the room.
